Freelancer invoicing guide

Getting paid is part of the job. This guide covers habits that reduce unpaid invoices. It is written for independent designers, developers, writers, and consultants who bill per project or per hour.

Agree on money before you start

A short email that states scope, rate, deposit, and due terms is easier to enforce than a verbal “we will figure it out”. If the client needs a formal purchase order, wait for it before the first large invoice.

Invoice promptly

Send the invoice the day the milestone is accepted, not weeks later. Finance teams close books on a schedule. A late invoice can sit until the next cycle even when the client is happy.

Use one number sequence

Skip cute numbers that reset every client. Auditors and you-in-six-months need a unique ID. InvoiceCraft Pro prefills a number; change the prefix if you already have a sequence in a spreadsheet.

Deposits and progress bills

For projects over a few days of work, a 30–50% deposit is common. Progress invoices should reference the same project name so the client can stack them. Do not start unpaid “phase two” while phase one is still outstanding unless you choose that risk on purpose.

Follow-up without heat

On the due date, a polite “checking that invoice INV-2026-014 arrived” is enough. A week later, ask if they need a different billing entity or PO. Keep the original PDF attached so nobody hunts email threads.

What not to put on an invoice

  • Internal arguments or chat screenshots.
  • Personal data the client did not ask you to store, such as extra passport scans.
  • Inflated hours you cannot back with a timesheet if asked.

Privacy while you bill

InvoiceCraft Pro keeps drafts in the browser so you are not uploading every client’s address to a random SaaS by default. Still, the PDF you email is a business record — treat it like any other confidential file.
